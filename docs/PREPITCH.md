## 0:20–1:20 · The problem: nobody knows what's going on
- **Customers are stuck and in the dark.** In Drenthe, TenneT's December 2025 study found **no room for feed-in**, so no new solar or wind parks can connect. Offtake is limited (§7). Customers wait without knowing when, why, or what they could do.
- **Dates keep moving.** The new Veenoord-Boerdijk station was needed in 2027 and is now expected in **2031** (§9). Every project behind it is affected, but the customer doesn't see that link.
- **Planners are in the dark too.** The province formally asked Enexis to show *when and where capacity becomes available*, with **red/orange flags on delays** and a **quarterly report on changes and required decisions**. Enexis replied that the investment plan is *a static document updated once every two years* (§9).
- **It will get harder:** peak load in Drenthe grows **×3 to ×5 by 2050** (§7).

## 1:20–3:10 · Our concept: one register, two views (show the mockup)
**Story (illustrative):** a developer wants to build a solar park with a battery near Emmen.
1. **Can I build here?** They click the location and see:
   - which substation area it falls in
   - its status today, for offtake and feed-in
   - the **planned grid upgrades with their real expected years**
   - a zoning flag
2. **When can I connect?** An **estimate shown as a range, with the reason**: "the bottleneck at your station is solved by project X, expected in 2031–33". And **what would make it sooner**: a battery, cable pooling, a time-block contract, or a group contract with neighbours (available from 2027).
3. **My projects.** For each of their projects: the application status, the next step, any missing paperwork, and a timeline. **They get a notification when the estimate changes**, for example because an upgrade is delayed.
4. **Planning view.** The same projects, added up per substation area and per year. It shows:
   - where and when capacity runs out
   - which upgrades are late (red/orange)
   - **what must be decided, by whom, by when**, e.g. "must be in the pMIEK by February to make the next investment plan"
   - the change report the province asked for

**The key idea:** customers get transparency **in return for** registering their plans early and keeping them current. That gives planners the up-to-date, location-specific data they lack. The form is based on the official **pMIEK project sheet** (§8).

## 3:10–3:40 · Why this is different
- The **capaciteitskaart** shows an area's colour *today*. The **investment plan** is *static, two-yearly and technical*. Neither tells a customer *when, why, and what next* for **their** project.
- Planners get plans late, through surveys and PDFs. **One register serves both sides.**
- We **don't** replace the capaciteitskaart, ETM or TNO's REV. We feed them. And we build **no scoring model**.

## 3:40–5:00 · Our questions for you (pick 4–5)
1. **Who are "the customers" you mean:** developers, companies, municipalities, or regional grid operators like Enexis and RENDO? Which group should we design for first?
2. **What do customers ask you most?** Their date, their status, their alternatives, or why it takes so long?
3. **How could a connection estimate be honest?** We plan to base it on the expected in-service year of the upgrade that relieves the customer's substation, plus their waiting-list position, with an uncertainty range. Is that how it works? What must *not* be shown, e.g. queue positions?
4. **Who would run the customer view?** Most customers connect via Enexis or RENDO, not TenneT. Should it be a joint grid-operator portal, or the province?
5. **Would customers share their plans early** in exchange for this transparency? What should stay confidential?
6. **Is there data we may use?** For example rough capacity per Drenthe station, or the steps and statuses of the connection process.
